## Start with what you need help with

Most visitors who look for a guide in Chengdu aren't really looking for transport. Taxis and ride-hailing apps cover that. What they want is interpretation: someone who can explain a panda's daily routine, the social rules of a teahouse, or why a 3,000-year-old bronze mask matters, and who can order dinner without guessing at the spice level.

So before you look at anyone, write one sentence describing the day you want. "A relaxed morning at the panda base and an afternoon in a park teahouse" is a very different brief from "two days in Jiuzhaigou with train transfers." The first needs a good explainer. The second needs someone who handles logistics, hotels and weather plans. That sentence will tell you which questions matter most.

## Know what "licensed" means in China

In China, guiding is a regulated job. Paid guides are expected to hold a national tour-guide qualification and be registered with a travel agency or guide-service organisation. The registration gives them an operational guide certificate. Industry write-ups describe paid guiding without one as unlicensed ("black guide") work, which leaves travelers with little recourse if something goes wrong.

The practical part for travelers is newer. On 30 June 2025, the Ministry of Culture and Tourism introduced a 2025 version of the electronic guide certificate and a physical **guide identity card**. According to the ministry's notice and Chinese press coverage:

- The card is vertical and colour-coded by grade: green (junior), blue (intermediate), orange (senior), red (special grade).
- The front shows the guide's photo, name, grade, certificate number, and **guiding language**.
- The back shows the registering organisation, the managing authority, the validity period, and a **QR code that travelers can scan** to check the electronic certificate.
- Old-style cards were valid alongside the new ones until 31 December 2025 and lapsed from 1 January 2026.

So in 2026 you can reasonably ask a guide to show the newer card, and you can scan its QR code yourself. A professional guide expects this and won't take offence. [VERIFY: what the QR code page looks like to a foreign phone without WeChat, and whether it opens in a normal browser.]

A licence is not the only sign of a good guide. Some useful companions, such as interpreters, students or friends of friends, aren't licensed guides and don't claim to be. That can be fine for an informal city walk. It matters more when someone is selling a multi-day itinerary, collecting money for tickets and hotels, or taking you somewhere remote.

## Questions to settle before you agree

A short written exchange protects both sides. Ask for:

1. **Identity and credentials.** Their name as it appears on the guide card, and a photo of the card's front if they hold one. Scan the QR code when you meet.
2. **The exact plan.** Meeting point (in Chinese characters), start and finish times, and the order of stops.
3. **What the price includes.** Guide fee, car and driver, fuel and parking, entrance tickets, sightseeing buses, meals, train fares. Agencies' own published ranges for a private guide in Chengdu run from roughly US$75 to US$450 a day, depending on hours, transport and group size. Those are sellers' figures, so get your own quote. [VERIFY before publishing: re-check the ranges on the cited pages.]
4. **Who books what.** Panda base, Sanxingdui and many other sites need real-name advance booking with your passport details. Agree whether you book or they do, and never send a passport photo through a channel you haven't verified.
5. **Payment and cancellation.** When payment is due, by what method, and what happens if it rains, a site closes, or you get sick.
6. **No-shopping terms.** If you don't want shop stops, say so in writing.
7. **Insurance and responsibility,** especially for mountain, high-altitude or multi-day trips.

## Red flags

- They won't say who they're registered with, or the name keeps changing.
- They want full payment up front for a short city day, through an unfamiliar link.
- The price is far below what similar services advertise, and there's a vague mention of "stops."
- The public profile has no dated posts from guiding days, only reposted scenery.
- They pressure you to decide quickly or to move the conversation to a private channel straight away.

None of these proves bad intent, but each one is a good reason to slow down.
